In 1975, a pediatrician named John Gall published a sardonic little book about why systems fail. Buried in it was an observation that has outlived the book, the decade, and most of the systems since built in defiance of it:

> A complex system that works is invariably found to have evolved from a simple system that worked. A complex system designed from scratch never works and cannot be patched up to make it work. You have to start over with a working simple system.

Read it again, slowly. Not "often". Not "tends to". *Invariably*. Gall wrote it as an empirical observation, the way a doctor records symptoms, and half a century of software has behaved like a clinical trial determined to confirm him.

## The grand design

Every organization eventually births one. The platform that will unify everything. The v2 that fixes all of v1's sins at once. Eighteen months of architecture diagrams, each box reasonable, the whole thing magnificent. It has an event bus before it has an event worth publishing.

The failure, when it comes, is rarely dramatic. The grand design does not explode; it just never quite works. Integration drags. Each fix reveals two more assumptions that reality declined to honor. The team patches, and patches the patches, and somewhere in year two everyone quietly understands what Gall said plainly: it cannot be patched into working, because it was never working. There is no working core to return to. The complexity did not grow around a living system; it was poured, all at once, like concrete around nothing.

## Why evolution wins

The mechanism is not mysterious. A simple system that works has been tested by the only reviewer that matters: contact with reality. Every user who bent it, every incident that scarred it, every workaround that got absorbed into it was a correction the designers could not have anticipated, because the information did not exist yet. It was generated by the system running.

A complex system designed from scratch contains thousands of decisions made before that information existed. Each one is a guess wearing the costume of a decision. Some guesses are wrong, which is survivable. What is not survivable is that they are load-bearing and interlocked, so no single wrong guess can be corrected without disturbing ten others.

Evolution is not slower than design. It only looks slower, because its failures are small and its progress is boring. The grand design fails in one large, expensive, career-ending motion. The evolved system fails in a thousand small, cheap, instructive ones.

## Wielding the law

Gall's Law is not a command to think small. The ambition can be as grand as you like. The *first version* cannot.

Start with a simple system that genuinely works: deployed, used, held to account by real traffic. Not a prototype, which is a demo wearing a lab coat, but the smallest honest version of the thing. Then grow it, one earned increment at a time, letting reality vote between steps. This is what evolutionary architecture formalizes, what MVPs gesture at, what every "walking skeleton" practice encodes. They are all Gall's Law with better marketing.

And when you inherit the other kind, the from-scratch cathedral that never quite worked, the law's harshest clause applies: you cannot patch it into working. The way out is the way Gall prescribed, back to the simplest version that demonstrably works, even when that version is embarrassingly small compared to the ambition. Especially then.

The next time a design review shows you the complete system, every service named, every interaction drawn, ask the only question the law requires: *which simple, working thing does this grow from?* If the answer is a diagram, you are not looking at a system. You are looking at a wish.

---

*Complexity that works is grown. Complexity that is installed merely waits to be discovered as failure.*
